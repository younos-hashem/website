The internet is a powerful thing.
When Gutenberg printed his Bible in the 15th century, he could hardly have envisioned that in a few hundred years any two-bit graduate student with a computer and some free time would be able to publish his thoughts for anyone in the world to access.

The story of writing has been one of slow democratization.
What began as a record-keeping tool, to be used only by government administrators, slowly spread among the upper classes before becoming widespread during the industrial revolution.
Now, almost everyone in developed countries can write<sup>1</sup>{{< sidenote >}}1\. Unfortunately, in some countries (particularly in sub-Saharan Africa), [many adults still cannot read and write](https://ourworldindata.org/literacy).{{</ sidenote >}}.
We are inundated today by information from all sides, and anyone anywhere can participate in the global information exchange---whether with a TikTok comment or a bestselling book.
I want to explore a little bit of the history of how we got here, and highlight a disturbing trend I see in how we use the internet, that of a growing institutionalization of the internet and technology more broadly<sup>2</sup>{{< sidenote >}}2\. Since the internet can apparently worsen in a lot of ways, it might also interest you to read about [enshittification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enshittification) and [Dead Internet theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_Internet_theory). Others have already written plenty on those, though.{{</ sidenote >}}.

### A Series of Tubes
The early internet was a strange place.
If you are, like me, an infant, you will have no recollection of what the online world was like 20–30 years ago; otherwise, you will have to bear with me as I attempt to reconstruct it.

First, a distinction: the internet is not the same as the web.
When you read one of these posts, you see a web site being delivered to you over the internet, but it's perfectly possible to use the internet without the web (e.g., an internet messaging app) or the web without the internet (e.g., by serving a web site on your local network without connecting to the outside world).
In the early days, there was no web, and use of the internet was largely limited to government, business, and academia for email and other communications.
One of the first online social networks to gain widespread use was [Usenet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Usenet).
Users on "newsgroups" organized around various topics post "articles"<sup>3</sup>{{< sidenote >}}3\. "Article" does not imply a formal publication, as in "news article," but could be any piece of text.{{</ sidenote >}} and respond with their own.
Its use in this era was limited primarily to people at universities (the only ones with email addresses), but it was in ways the precursor to internet forums and social media.

The internet and World Wide Web became publicly available in the early ’90s.
Public adoption picked up quickly, and soon after came the dot-com boom, in which one could become rich by taking a decent business and adding ".com" to the end of its name.
(For instance, Mark Cuban made his fortune by selling Broadcast.com to Yahoo for $5 billion in 1999 ($10 billion in 2026 dollars). Yahoo shut the service down three years later.)

Despite the dot-com crash, the internet, of course, became ubiquitous.
In this era of the internet—before social media—I want to pay particular emphasis to blogs and internet forums.

Blogs gave people the ability to share their ideas with the world.
What started as a way for people to document interesting places on the web eventually became a sort of diary, and in the early 2000s the "blogosphere" took shape.
Many of these early blogs were hosted on dedicated services like Blogger, which made creating a blog easy but also had the downside of homogeneity.
Many blogs looked the same, and all sites were hosted on the Blogger servers (later Google, after an acquisition).
WordPress, an open-source toolset for blog publishing, would launch later, expanding the number of options.

Early forums were perhaps the most widespread form of mass social networking before modern social media platforms.
Evolving out of Usenet and physical bulletin boards, they allowed users to engage in long, threaded conversations and were organized around a central topic—a type of car, a video game, a profession.
Two key features of these forums are that they are open and decentralized: Anyone can access and read the forum, and the forums for, say, the Toyota Corolla and the Python programming language are totally independent sites.
These aspects make forums an essentially democratic technology.
The latter, in particular, might seem a bit minor, but it is important, protecting the broader forum network from the bad decisions of any one forum's maintainers.
Of course, these forums still exist, but typically around specialized topics and with drastically reduced user counts<sup>4</sup>{{< sidenote >}}4\. For instance, when I visit the [Python forums](https://python-forum.io/), I am the only user browsing, and there seem to be 1–2 posts per day, if that.{{</ sidenote >}}.
They have largely been supplanted by modern social media.

Mark Zuckerberg launched Facebook in 2004.
Other social media platforms existed before, but none would be as long-lasting or as impactful as Facebook.
Facebook's ability to connect people personally and directly, even if they did not necessarily know each other, was legitimately transformational.
Usenet and the early forums brought people together, but always did so in a specific context.
With Facebook, the context became universal, and, without barriers, the network could grow far wider than before.

In the decade after Facebook's launch, there was some discussion of its potential as a powerful democratic force.
In particular, during the Arab Spring, protestors used Facebook to organize collective action and to document the crimes of their oppressive governments.
Unfortunately, this was largely a failure.
The only place where the Arab Spring could be said to have succeeded was in Tunisia, which has suffered from significant democratic backsliding in the years since.
Syria is perhaps another example, but only after 14 grueling years of civil war that have left the country in devastation.

### The Walls Close In
The internet today is a very different place than it was 20 or 30 years ago.
Gradually, there has been a shift from the decentralized blog- and forum-based structure of the early internet to one dominated by a few large companies.
This has happened in several distinct ways.

Blogs, though they seem to have dormant for a while, have made a bit of a resurgence lately.
Unfortunately, many if not most modern blogs are hosted on Substack or Medium.
For one thing, these platforms make it hard or impossible to customize your blog beyond the very basic of changes.
This might seem like a minor aesthetic preference, but a site's design can tell you a lot about the person who made it, as in the figure above.
These platforms strip people of their expressive power.
Also, the platforms are closed off. (Medium is particularly bad here.)
Writers on Medium are beholden to its recommendation algorithm.
If Substack decides to increase the cut of your money it takes—too bad!

Forums have died a long, slow death.
In a sense, this is natural.
If I want a forum to discuss, say, birch trees, why should I go to the trouble of setting up the entire forum infrastructure when I can do it on Reddit in a few clicks instead?<sup>5</sup>{{< sidenote >}}5\. [/r/Birchtrees does exist](https://www.reddit.com/r/Birchtrees/), but it's about as inactive as you might imagine.{{</ sidenote >}}
For things like neighborhood groups, the contrast is even starker.
It makes almost no sense to host a dedicated forum for your hyperlocal community compared to using Facebook or a platform like Nextdoor.
This centralization robs people of control in two very important ways.
First, users have little control over their data, which might be [used to influence an election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facebook%E2%80%93Cambridge_Analytica_data_scandal) or be [handed over to the government](https://transparency.meta.com/reports/government-data-requests/).
Also, content moderation is centralized and often poor.
Famously, [Facebook was used](https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2022/09/myanmar-facebooks-systems-promoted-violence-against-rohingya-meta-owes-reparations-new-report/) to promote violence and hateful rhetoric against Myanmar's Rohingya minority during an ethnic cleansing campaign against that group.
Facebook's moderation systems failed to clamp down on this activity, and its recommendation algorithms promoted it.
Of course, this kind of content can (and does) still appear on small, independent platforms, but without the global connectivity and the recommendation algorithm, its reach is much more limited.

Another, more silent, killer of the forum has been Discord.
Discord began as a messaging and voice chatting application for gamers, but it has grown to include many other communities.
Often, these can include groups that might share information about hobbies or technical topics.
When these communities move from the open web to a closed platform, that knowledge goes with them.
Yes, knowledge on forums can die if the forum dies, but at least it is public, indexed by search engines, and able to be archived.
Discord has no such advantage.

Finally, much of the technology we use on a daily basis is also becoming less open.
Apple is one of the worst offenders here, with its famous "walled garden" ecosystem that is designed to work extremely well when using only Apple products and very little or not at all in any other case.
Google recently announced [a change to Android](https://keepandroidopen.org/) that would require every app developer to register with Google—including paying a fee and giving the company government identification—before the app can be installed on an Android device.
Android's flexibility and openness has long been one of its selling points.
The walls are closing in.

So what are we to do?
For people with some technical know-how, free time, and (importantly) willingness to put in the work, there are alternatives.
For instance, on my phone, I use [LineageOS](https://lineageos.org/), an operating system based on Android (which is open-source) that does not come pre-installed with any of Google's apps.
However, this is really not practical for most people.
Getting some basic functionality to work took multiple hours of trawling through forums (some of the few still active) and asking LLMs to help fix issues, and some things still do not work properly<sup>6</sup>{{< sidenote >}}6\. One such feature is [RCS messaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rich_Communication_Services), which Google promoted as an open standard but restricts from working on Android devices not using its distribution.{{</ sidenote >}}.

In general, the open-source community has great tooling but is not so good at adapting that tooling for broader use.
For instance, compare Twitter/X with its largest open-source counterpart, Mastodon.
To start using Twitter, all you have to do is create an account.
To do so on Mastodon, you have to (a) understand how Mastodon works (not a trivial task!); (b) pick a server to create an account on; and (c) find interesting accounts from other servers to diversify your feed.

I do think, though, that there is a case for optimism.
Much of the web and internet already runs on open-source tooling.
For instance, WordPress, which is open-source, underlies many websites.
On the development side, most code is compiled with open-source compilers, uses Git for version control, and is often developed on Linux machines.
This was not at all the case in the early era of computing, when computer code was written to run on specific, proprietary hardware and was not easily transferable to other devices.
Such a world is unimaginable to modern developers.

How can the success of open-source software on the development side be replicated on the consumer end?
For development, there is a strong incentive for open tooling: It lowers development costs for everyone, so the few companies that benefit from closed tooling are unable to hold the rest of the community back.
The incentive structure is different for consumer-facing technology, where the user experience is not immediately affected meaningfully by the openness of the software.
One promising route is the so-called open-core model.
In this framework, the core part of a piece of software is kept free and open-source, with development support from a for-profit company.
This company then offers additional (closed-source) features or dedicated customer support for a fee.
Open-source purists will not like this solution, but it in principle addresses both the ease-of-use concern and the openness one.
Importantly, this model or variants of it have worked in the past. Red Hat Enterprise Linux, which runs many corporate servers, follows this model. (This is more "enterprise" than "consumer," but it's close enough.) Gaming on Linux was famously atrocious until Valve threw its weight behind Wine in the form of [Proton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton_\(software\)).

In ways, I'm a bit of an open-source idealist.
Of course, I'm not under any delusions that people will start messaging on Signal or tweeting on Mastodon en masse (though I would love if they did).
But I think that, when you look at the history, the story of open-source software has been one of slow growth and increasing ease-of-use.
I'm hopeful that, despite some opposing forces, these trends can continue.
